The Sahara Desert yielded a 4.1 billion‑year‑old rock that scientists have confirmed originated on Mars. Named Teghaza 001, the 800‑gram meteorite was blasted off the Red Planet by an ancient impact and landed on Earth in 2022. Its mineral mix and preserved zircon crystals record interactions between rock and liquid water shortly after Mars formed. Because the sample comes from a region of the Martian crust never before studied, it could reshape our picture of early planetary water cycles.

<Callout type="takeaway">Teghaza 001 is a rare Martian crust fragment that directly records water activity over 4 billion years ago.</Callout>

## What happened
Teghaza 001 was uncovered on the salt plain of Teghaza in Mali, where it was first recognized as a meteorite in 2022. Laboratory analysis showed it formed more than 4.1 billion years ago and weighs roughly 800 grams, placing it among the three oldest Martian meteorites known.

Further study revealed a distinctive blend of igneous minerals and textures that preserve evidence of water alteration. Researchers identified 13 plausible source regions on Mars, many of which correspond to the southern highlands observed by orbiting spacecraft. The rock’s oxygen isotopes and rubidium‑strontium ages confirm its Martian origin and its early interaction with liquid water.

## Why it matters
The meteorite provides the first direct sample of a Martian crustal segment that has never been examined, offering a tangible record of early water–rock chemistry. Its age pushes the timeline for liquid water on Mars further back, supporting models of a thicker early atmosphere and a more active hydrologic cycle. Understanding these processes informs the search for past habitability and guides future sample‑return missions that aim to target similar ancient terrains.

## How to think about it
Treat Teghaza 001 as a unique puzzle piece rather than a definitive map of early Mars. Combine its geochemical data with orbital observations to refine models of where water persisted. Prioritize comparative studies with other Martian meteorites and upcoming rover analyses to assess whether the water signatures are localized or widespread. For educators and enthusiasts, use the find to illustrate how a single rock can reshape planetary narratives while reminding readers of the limits of single‑sample inference.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that water was globally abundant on early Mars based solely on this one meteorite; broader context is essential.</Callout>
