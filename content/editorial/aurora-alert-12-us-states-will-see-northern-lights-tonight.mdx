Tonight, the sky over parts of the United States may glow with a phenomenon that many have only seen in the far north. A geomagnetic storm, predicted by NOAA’s Space Weather Prediction Center, is expected to lift auroral activity to lower latitudes. This could allow 12 states to witness the Northern Lights for the first time in years. The event is a reminder of the dynamic relationship between solar activity and Earth’s atmosphere.

## What happened
NOAA’s forecast issued on Friday indicates a moderate geomagnetic storm with a Kp index reaching 5 by evening. The forecast lists 12 states—Arizona, Colorado, Idaho, Kansas, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Dakota, and Utah—as having a chance of auroral visibility. The storm is driven by an increased flux of charged particles from the Sun, which interact with Earth’s magnetosphere.

## Why it matters
Auroras are not just a visual spectacle; they are a natural laboratory for studying magnetospheric physics and space weather. For the public, the event offers a unique educational opportunity and a potential boost to local tourism. However, geomagnetic storms can also pose risks to satellite operations, radio communications, and power grids, especially if the storm intensifies.
