China unveiled a four‑mission lunar campaign at the 7th Manned Space Symposium, aiming for a crewed touchdown before 2030. The plan starts with a 2027 Long March 10A test flight, followed by an uncrewed lunar descent in 2028, a dual‑launch rehearsal in 2029, and a crewed landing by the end of 2030. NASA’s Artemis program still targets a 2028 crewed landing, putting both agencies on a tight, overlapping schedule. The rivalry reshapes the near‑term dynamics of lunar exploration and international space policy.

<Callout type="takeaway">China’s accelerated lunar schedule forces NASA to reassess its Artemis timeline.</Callout>

## What happened
China’s roadmap details a sequential progression: 2027 sees the maiden flight of Long March 10A carrying the Mengzhou cargo capsule to low‑Earth orbit for systems validation. In 2028 the heavier Long March 10 will launch the Lanyue lander on an uncrewed lunar descent and ascent test, demonstrating propulsion and guidance capabilities. 2029 involves two Long March 10 launches for a full‑scale lunar‑orbit rehearsal, including rendezvous and docking, and the final dual‑launch crewed mission is slated for before the end of 2030.

NASA’s Artemis program, backed by the U.S. government and commercial partners, remains on track for a crewed lunar landing in 2028, following the Artemis III mission that will deliver astronauts to the Moon’s south pole. The overlapping timelines have heightened scrutiny of budget, technology readiness, and international cooperation.

## Why it matters
The parallel schedules intensify geopolitical competition, as each nation seeks leadership in lunar science, resource utilization, and deep‑space capabilities. Success for China would expand its long‑term presence beyond low‑Earth orbit, while a timely Artemis landing would reaffirm U.S. leadership and sustain commercial lunar initiatives. The race also influences partner nations’ decisions on where to invest their limited space budgets, potentially fragmenting collaborative efforts.

## How to think about it
Policymakers should treat the timelines as provisional and build flexibility into funding streams, allowing re‑allocation if either program slips. Industry stakeholders can diversify by developing modular lunar assets that serve both national and commercial customers. The public and scientific community benefit from advocating for shared standards and data‑exchange protocols that keep cooperation viable even amid competition.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the 2028–2030 dates are fixed; technical setbacks could shift both programs by several years.</Callout>
