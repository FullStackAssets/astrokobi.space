Astronomers have just mapped the molecular gas in a distant galaxy group and discovered that the galaxies’ fuel for new stars is slashed by more than half. The team focused on COSMOS‑Gr30, a dense collection of at least 44 galaxies sitting 7 billion light‑years away at the crossroads of several cosmic‑web filaments. Using the NOEMA interferometer and the IRAM 30 m telescope, they measured carbon‑monoxide emission as a proxy for molecular hydrogen. The result links the harsh group environment directly to the long‑term decline of star formation across the universe.

<Callout type="takeaway">Dense group environments can strip away a galaxy’s molecular gas, cutting its star‑forming fuel by over 50%.</Callout>

## What happened
The researchers pointed NOEMA and the IRAM 30 m dish at COSMOS‑Gr30, a galaxy group observed at a redshift that corresponds to roughly 7 billion light‑years. By detecting CO line emission they inferred the amount of molecular hydrogen, the raw material for star formation, in each of the group’s members. The group lies at the intersection of multiple filaments of the cosmic web, making it one of the densest environments known at that epoch.

When they compared the CO‑derived gas masses to a control sample of isolated galaxies with similar stellar masses, the group galaxies showed gas reservoirs reduced by more than half on average. This depletion is far larger than what can be explained by internal processes alone, pointing to external mechanisms such as tidal stripping and ram‑pressure from the hot intra‑group medium.

## Why it matters
The finding provides direct observational evidence that environment plays a dominant role in quenching star formation after the universe’s peak 10 billion years ago. By removing the molecular gas supply, dense groups accelerate the transition of galaxies from active to passive, reshaping the population of galaxies we see today. Models of galaxy evolution must therefore incorporate environmental stripping alongside internal feedback to reproduce the observed decline in cosmic star‑formation rate.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a galaxy’s future star‑forming potential, start by assessing its environment: density of nearby companions, location within filaments, and presence of hot intra‑group gas. If a galaxy resides in a crowded group, factor in likely gas loss from tidal forces and ram‑pressure stripping before applying internal‑feedback models. For observers, prioritize CO observations of galaxies across a range of environments to build a statistical picture of how universal this depletion effect is.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that all galaxy groups exhibit the same level of gas depletion without additional observations; the effect may vary with group mass, age, and filament connectivity.</Callout>
