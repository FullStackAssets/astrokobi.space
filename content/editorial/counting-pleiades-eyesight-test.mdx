The Pleiades open up each November as a natural eye‑test for anyone willing to look up. By counting the stars you can see without aid, you get a quick sense of how dark the sky is and how sharp your vision feels. The cluster is bright enough to find without equipment, yet subtle enough to reveal differences in visual acuity and light‑pollution levels. Understanding what you see – or don’t see – can deepen your appreciation of the night sky and your own eyesight.

<Callout type="takeaway">Counting the naked‑eye stars in the Pleiades offers a fast, informal gauge of visual acuity and sky darkness.</Callout>

## What happened
The Pleiades (Messier 45) sit about 444 light‑years away in Taurus and are visible to the naked eye for most mid‑northern latitudes in November. Under reasonably dark conditions observers typically see six of the brightest members, while the classic “Seven Sisters” label can mislead; only under very dark skies do many report seven or more stars. The cluster’s age is roughly 100 million years, making its stars relatively young and luminous.

The author of the article conducts a personal, unscientific test each autumn: step outside, locate the Pleiades, and count the visible points of light. The count can shift within minutes as eyes adapt, as light‑pollution changes, or as the cluster rises higher in the sky, improving atmospheric clarity.

## Why it matters
The exercise highlights two intertwined factors: human visual performance and environmental sky quality. A higher star count suggests both good eyesight and low light‑pollution, encouraging readers to seek darker sites for stargazing. Conversely, a low count can prompt awareness of local light‑glow and the need for eye‑care habits like dark adaptation. While not a medical diagnostic, the test serves as an accessible reminder that protecting both eyes and night‑sky environments benefits astronomy and health.

## How to think about it
Treat the Pleiades count as a qualitative checkpoint rather than a precise metric. First, give your eyes 10‑15 minutes to adapt in darkness before counting. Second, note the number of stars you see and compare it with a known baseline (six under typical suburban skies, seven or more under truly dark sites). Third, use the result to decide whether to seek darker observing locations or to schedule an eye‑exam if you notice a sudden decline. Over time, tracking your counts can reveal trends in both personal vision and local sky quality.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on this exercise as a medical diagnosis; significant vision changes should be evaluated by an eye‑care professional.</Callout>
