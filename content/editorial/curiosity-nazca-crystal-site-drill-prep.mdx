Curiosity’s latest high‑resolution snapshot shows a field of disk‑shaped crystal formations at a newly identified dig site informally called “Nazca.” The image, taken on Oct. 18, 2026 with the rover’s Mars Hand Lens Imager, reveals multiple roughly circular features that scientists suspect are mineral crystals or their pseudomorphs. Researchers will now analyze the surface composition in detail before committing to a drill, hoping the crystals’ size and texture can unlock clues about past temperatures and fluid activity. Understanding these clues is crucial for piecing together Mars’ climatic history and assessing the habitability of ancient environments. This careful pre‑drill characterization could also guide future sample‑return strategies.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Nazca crystals could reveal Mars’ ancient temperature and fluid history before drilling.</Callout>

## What happened
The Curiosity rover used its Mars Hand Lens Imager (MAHLI) on Oct. 18, 2026 to capture a composite close‑up of the Nazca target. MAHLI, mounted on the rover’s robotic arm turret, creates focused images by stacking multiple exposures taken at slightly different distances. The resulting picture shows several disk‑shaped structures that resemble mineral crystals, some appearing as needle‑like extensions.

Scientists interpret the “discs and needles” texture as evidence of crystal growth, possibly influenced by the temperature and salinity of fluids that once percolated through the rock. However, they caution that these crystals may have been altered after formation; replacement minerals—pseudomorphs—can retain the original shape while changing composition, complicating direct interpretation.

The team plans to conduct spectroscopic and chemical analyses of the surface material before drilling deeper. By comparing surface and subsurface data, they aim to determine whether the observed crystals are primary formations or later replacements, which will inform the selection of drilling depth and sampling strategy.

## Why it matters
If the crystal sizes indeed reflect formation temperatures, they could provide a rare window into the thermal conditions of Mars’ past, helping to refine models of its climate evolution. The presence of salts and fluids inferred from the crystals also bears on the planet’s potential to have supported life. Moreover, understanding the degree of pseudomorph alteration will improve the reliability of future sample‑return missions, ensuring that collected material truly represents ancient environments rather than later overprints.

## How to think about it
Treat the Nazca observations as a layered puzzle: first, catalog the visual textures; second, cross‑reference MAHLI data with spectroscopic readings to infer composition; third, model possible temperature‑fluid scenarios that could produce the observed crystal sizes. Use this hierarchy to prioritize drilling locations that maximize scientific return while minimizing the chance of sampling altered material. In practice, adopt a “surface‑first, subsurface‑later” workflow, updating drill plans as each new data set refines the geological narrative.
