When the James Webb Space Telescope trained on interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS in late December 2025, it detected methane that is unusually enriched in deuterium. The measured deuterium‑to‑hydrogen (D/H) ratio in the comet’s methane is about 3.2 %, roughly thirteen times the value found in comet 67P/Churyumov‑Gerasimenko. This signal is faint, buried beneath brighter infrared features, but careful modeling pulled it into view. The result offers a direct chemical clue to the temperature and environment where the comet’s ices first formed. Understanding that birthplace helps astronomers piece together how planetary systems assemble beyond our own.

<Callout type="takeaway">3I/ATLAS’s methane carries a deuterium signature that points to a birth in an extremely cold, distant region.</Callout>

## What happened
JWST observed 3I/ATLAS on 22–23 December 2025 when the comet was 2.4 AU from the Sun. Infrared spectra of the coma revealed the vibrational bands of methane and a much weaker set belonging to CH₃D, the deuterated isotopologue. By fitting and subtracting contributions from water, carbon monoxide and other gases, the team isolated the CH₃D feature and derived a D/H ratio of roughly 3.2 % in methane.

For comparison, the D/H ratio in methane measured for comet 67P was about 0.25 %, making 3I/ATLAS’s value thirteen times larger. The authors note that this is the highest methane D/H ratio recorded in any solar‑system or interstellar comet to date.

## Why it matters
A high deuterium fraction in methane is a strong indicator of formation at temperatures below ~30 K, where deuterium preferentially incorporates into icy molecules. This suggests that 3I/ATLAS’s ices condensed in an ultra‑cold region of another star’s protoplanetary disk—or even in the pre‑stellar molecular cloud that birthed that system. The finding expands the chemical diversity we know exists beyond the Solar System and provides a benchmark for models of disk chemistry, planetesimal formation, and the delivery of volatiles to nascent worlds.

## How to think about it
Treat the methane D/H ratio as a temperature proxy, but always cross‑check with other isotopic and molecular markers such as water D/H or CO isotopologues. Compare the observed value against chemical models of cold‑disk and cloud chemistry to infer likely formation zones. When new interstellar comets appear, prioritize high‑resolution infrared spectroscopy early in their inbound trajectory to capture similarly faint signatures before solar heating modifies the coma.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the measured D/H ratio directly reflects the pristine formation value; coma chemistry and observational uncertainties can bias the result.</Callout>
