When humanity first turned its radio dishes toward the stars, we listened—but soon some scientists argued we should also speak. The practice of deliberately sending messages to potential extraterrestrial intelligences, now called METI, began in earnest during the 1960s, building on the momentum of the first SETI experiment, Project Ozma. Early METI projects such as the 1974 Arecibo message and the 1975 Cosmic Call experiments marked the transition from passive listening to active outreach. These initiatives sparked debate about the scientific, ethical, and security implications of announcing our presence to an unknown audience.

<Callout type="takeaway">METI turned humanity from a silent observer into an active broadcaster, igniting both excitement and controversy.</Callout>

## What happened
The idea of sending intentional signals emerged from discussions about the “Great Silence” and the so‑called Fermi paradox, which highlighted the puzzling lack of detectable extraterrestrial activity despite the vastness of the cosmos. In the 1960s, researchers proposed that active transmission could complement passive listening, arguing that a deliberate beacon might be more likely to attract attention.

The first high‑profile METI effort was the Arecibo message in 1974, a binary transmission beamed toward the globular cluster M13 using the newly upgraded Arecibo radio telescope. A year later, the Cosmic Call experiment sent a series of encoded pulses toward nearby Sun‑like stars, testing both the technical feasibility of interstellar messaging and the public’s reaction to such outreach. These early experiments demonstrated that humanity could encode and transmit information across light‑years, laying the groundwork for later, more sophisticated METI projects.

## Why it matters
Active messaging reshapes the SETI paradigm by shifting humanity from a passive listener to a participant in a potential interstellar dialogue. If a signal were received, it could provide the first direct evidence of intelligent life, transforming science, philosophy, and society. Conversely, broadcasting our existence raises concerns about security, cultural impact, and the ethical responsibility of representing Earth without global consensus.

## How to think about it
Treat METI as a policy‑driven scientific activity. First, assess the technical feasibility and signal detectability for target stars. Second, convene a broad, interdisciplinary panel—including astronomers, ethicists, security experts, and representatives from diverse cultures—to review proposed messages. Third, adopt transparent governance structures that require international consensus before any transmission. Finally, view each METI attempt as an experiment: define clear objectives, measurable outcomes, and a plan for post‑transmission analysis.

<Callout type="warning">Do not launch a METI transmission without documented international review and a clear risk‑assessment framework.</Callout>
