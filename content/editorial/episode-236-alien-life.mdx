The latest episode of This Week In Space, released on November 14, 2026, brings together hosts Tariq Malik and Rod Pyle with Washington University researcher Mikhail Tikhonov. The conversation turns away from classic sci‑fi depictions of alien beings and asks what truly counts as life beyond Earth. Tikhonov proposes a minimalist definition: any system that takes in energy, transforms it, and replicates itself. This shift could reshape how scientists design future missions and interpret ambiguous signals.
<Callout type="takeaway">Life may be any energy‑transforming, self‑replicating system, not just carbon‑based organisms.</Callout>
## What happened
In the 236th episode, the hosts explored the limits of our assumptions about extraterrestrial biology. They noted that popular culture often defaults to humanoid or exotic creature tropes, while mainstream astrobiology typically focuses on carbon‑based microbes.
Tikhonov argued that a functional definition—energy intake, transformation, and replication—captures a broader spectrum of possibilities, from silicon‑based chemistries to plasma‑based structures. He emphasized that this definition is operational for detection: instruments should look for energy gradients and self‑assembly signatures rather than specific molecular markers.
The episode also referenced SpaceX’s upcoming Starship double‑catch test, reminding listeners that expanding our technological reach will provide more platforms for unconventional life searches.
## Why it matters
If the definition of life expands, mission planners may prioritize instruments that monitor metabolic‑like energy flows, widening the target list for moons such as Europa or Titan. A broader view also influences funding priorities, encouraging interdisciplinary work between physicists, chemists, and computer scientists. Ultimately, it could alter the philosophical narrative of humanity’s place in the cosmos, moving from a carbon‑centric view to a universal one.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a candidate environment, first ask whether there is a sustained energy source, then look for evidence of transformation (e.g., chemical gradients) and finally seek signs of replication (patterns that repeat or grow). Apply this three‑step framework alongside traditional markers to avoid overlooking exotic possibilities while maintaining scientific rigor.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume any anomalous signal is biological without independent verification of energy flow and replication mechanisms.</Callout>
