On November 15, 2026 the European Space Agency announced that its Euclid telescope has catalogued 31 quasars from the Universe’s first billion years, including two that shine with the power of a trillion Suns. These objects existed when the cosmos was only 670 million years old—about five percent of its current age. Detecting such faint, distant beacons pushes the limits of observational astronomy and opens a new window onto the birth of supermassive black holes. The discovery reshapes our picture of how quickly massive galaxies assembled in the early Universe. It also demonstrates Euclid’s capability to survey vast swaths of sky for the faintest signals.

<Callout type="takeaway">The earliest quasars reveal that supermassive black holes grew to enormous size within the first few hundred million years.</Callout>

## What happened
Euclid, launched in 2023, has surveyed an unprecedented area of the sky with infrared imaging and spectroscopy, allowing it to spot extremely faint, high‑redshift sources. The mission’s latest data release lists 31 quasars that existed when the Universe was roughly 670 million years old, and two of those emit as much light as a trillion Suns.

Quasars are powered by gas spiralling into supermassive black holes, releasing prodigious energy that can outshine their host galaxies. Historically, only the very brightest early quasars were detectable, biasing our view of the nascent black‑hole population. Euclid’s sensitivity has now uncovered many fainter members, expanding the known diversity of early active galactic nuclei.

Finding such objects is difficult because their light is both extremely redshifted and intrinsically faint, making them easy to confuse with nearer stars. Sophisticated colour‑selection techniques and spectroscopic confirmation were essential to verify the new sample.

## Why it matters
The presence of trillion‑sun quasars only 670 million years after the Big Bang challenges conventional models of black‑hole growth, which struggle to produce masses of billions of solar masses so quickly. Their abundance also informs theories of early galaxy formation, suggesting that massive structures assembled earlier than many simulations predict. Moreover, the expanded sample of fainter quasars provides a more representative view of the early active‑galaxy population, refining estimates of the ionising radiation that re‑ionised the intergalactic medium.

## How to think about it
Treat the new quasars as anchor points for early‑universe models rather than definitive proof of any single formation pathway. Compare their observed properties with predictions from hydrodynamic simulations, noting where discrepancies arise. When incorporating them into broader cosmological analyses, account for selection effects: Euclid’s survey depth favours certain redshifts and luminosities. Finally, use the fainter objects to probe the typical growth environment of early black holes, while reserving the extreme, trillion‑sun sources for studying the most rapid growth episodes.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the measured luminosities directly translate to black‑hole masses without correcting for possible gravitational lensing or dust attenuation.</Callout>
