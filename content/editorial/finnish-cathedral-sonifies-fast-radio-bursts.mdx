Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are millisecond‑long flashes of radio energy from distant galaxies, invisible to the eye and unheard by the ear.  A Finnish composer has transformed the entire CHIME catalog of FRBs into an acoustic installation inside Oulu Cathedral, where a choir will perform alongside the sounds on Nov. 21.  The project bridges astrophysics and art, turning abstract data into a sensory experience that can be heard by anyone who visits.  By mapping burst properties to pitch, duration, and spatial placement, the installation lets listeners sense the diversity of cosmic explosions that have been recorded over the past decade.

<Callout type="takeaway">Turning radio data into sound lets the public experience FRBs in a way that visualizations alone cannot convey.</Callout>

## What happened
The Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) recorded 4,539 bursts from 3,641 sources between 2018 and 2023, vastly expanding the known FRB population.  Composer Andrew Melchior, in collaboration with MIT physicist Kiyoshi Masui, converted each burst’s frequency, intensity, and arrival time into musical parameters, creating a 12‑speaker soundscape that fills the cathedral.  The installation, titled “The Logos,” has been playing daily since early April and will run through the end of the year, culminating in a live choir performance on Nov. 21.

## Why it matters
Sonifying FRBs provides an alternative pathway for public engagement with cutting‑edge astrophysics, making abstract, invisible phenomena tangible.  It also highlights the sheer volume of data modern radio surveys generate, encouraging interdisciplinary approaches to data interpretation.  For scientists, hearing patterns may inspire new ways to spot anomalies or classify bursts, while for artists it demonstrates how scientific rigor can fuel creative expression.

## How to think about it
Treat the sonification as a complementary narrative, not a substitute for scientific analysis.  Listen for recurring motifs that might hint at repeating sources, then cross‑reference those patterns with the original radio data.  Use the experience as a springboard: ask what the pitch, volume, or timing represents, and explore the underlying astrophysical parameters.  In educational settings, pair the audio with visual plots so learners can map sound to spectra, reinforcing the multi‑modal nature of modern science.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the musical interpretation reflects the true physical intensity of each burst; the mapping is an artistic choice, not a direct measurement.</Callout>
