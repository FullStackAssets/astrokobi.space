On November 5, 2026, ESA announced that its Hera spacecraft has taken its first close‑up images of the binary asteroid system Didymos and its moon Dimorphos. The spacecraft was only about 60 km from Didymos when it snapped the view, revealing the far side of the primary that DART never saw. Dimorphos appears partially in the shadow of Didymos, offering a rare perspective on the impact site created by NASA’s 2022 DART mission. These observations come just weeks before Hera begins detailed mapping of the system, a key step in evaluating planetary‑defense techniques. The data will help scientists compare pre‑impact and post‑impact conditions, improving models of asteroid response to kinetic impacts.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hera’s first close‑up images give the first look at the side of Didymos and Dimorphos that DART never observed, crucial for assessing the impact’s effects.</Callout>

## What happened
ESA’s Hera was roughly 37 miles (60 km) from the Didymos system when it captured the first high‑resolution image, showing the opposite hemisphere of Didymos from the one DART photographed. The picture reveals the familiar central ridge along Didymos’s equator, but also a landscape dotted with new boulders, craters, and linear grooves that differ from the previously seen side. Dimorphos is visible in the larger asteroid’s shadow, hinting at its shape and the extent of the crater left by the DART impact.

Mission scientists noted that some of the newly observed features could be material ejected from Dimorphos that traveled the roughly 1 km gap between the bodies, a hypothesis that will guide upcoming mapping efforts. ESA also reminded the public that a full set of detailed images will be released on November 25, after Hera completes its close‑approach maneuvers.

## Why it matters
The new perspective allows researchers to directly compare the pre‑impact surface of Didymos with the post‑impact environment of Dimorphos, testing the effectiveness of kinetic‑impact deflection—a cornerstone of planetary‑defense strategy. Understanding how ejecta redistribute across a binary system informs future mission designs and risk assessments for Earth‑crossing asteroids. Moreover, the geological diversity now visible on Didymos expands our knowledge of small‑body formation and evolution, data that are essential for both scientific inquiry and practical mitigation planning.

## How to think about it
Treat Hera’s data as a baseline rather than a final verdict. Compare the new morphology with DART’s pre‑impact images to isolate changes directly attributable to the impact. Use the observed ejecta patterns to refine numerical models of momentum transfer, then apply those models to larger, potentially hazardous asteroids. Finally, consider the broader context: binary asteroids are common, so lessons from Didymos‑Dimorphos will shape how we protect Earth from a range of threat scenarios.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the single close‑up image fully characterizes the entire system; further mapping and analysis are required before drawing definitive conclusions.</Callout>
