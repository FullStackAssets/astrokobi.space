On Nov. 5, ESA released a stunning image that gives humanity its first direct look at the side of the Didymos binary asteroid system that DART never saw. The European Space Agency’s Hera spacecraft, now orbiting the pair, captured the view while surveying the aftermath of NASA’s 2022 DART impact on Dimorphos. These fresh details of boulders, fresh craters and ridge patterns provide the most concrete evidence yet of how a kinetic impact reshapes a small body, a cornerstone for planetary‑defense planning.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hera’s close‑up proves that even a modest impact can dramatically remodel an asteroid’s surface, validating kinetic deflection concepts.</Callout>

## What happened
Hera’s camera recorded Didymos, a roughly 2,560‑foot (780 m) primary, and a sliver of its 530‑foot (160 m) moonlet Dimorphos. The image shows a central equatorial ridge unchanged, but new boulders, craters and groove patterns scattered across the surface.

The DART spacecraft struck Dimorphos on 26 Sept 2022, shortening its orbital period from about 11 hours 55 minutes to roughly 11 hours 22 minutes – a reduction of ~33 minutes. The impact also slowed Dimorphos by about 2.7 mm s⁻¹ relative to its pre‑impact velocity.

Material ejected by the impact appears to have traveled the ~1 km gap between the bodies, depositing debris on Didymos and creating the observed surface modifications.

## Why it matters
The observations give scientists concrete data on how kinetic‑impact deflection reshapes an asteroid, confirming that even modest velocity changes can alter an orbit enough to avert a collision given sufficient warning. This real‑world evidence strengthens the case for future impactor missions as a practical planetary‑defense tool and refines models that predict debris distribution and long‑term orbital evolution.

## How to think about it
Treat the Hera findings as a calibration point rather than a universal rule. Policy makers should pair kinetic‑deflection plans with robust detection networks to guarantee early warning, while scientists should incorporate the observed boulder and crater formation rates into simulation tools. For each target asteroid, assess composition, size and structural integrity before deciding on impactor mass and velocity.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the surface changes seen at Didymos will be identical for all near‑Earth asteroids; each body’s composition and structure can produce very different responses to an impact.</Callout>
