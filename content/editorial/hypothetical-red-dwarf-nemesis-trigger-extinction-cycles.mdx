Every 26 million years the fossil record shows a pronounced spike in mass extinctions, a pattern that has puzzled paleontologists for decades.  A new interview with physicist Richard Muller revives an older idea: a low‑mass red dwarf, nicknamed Nemesis, may orbit the Sun and periodically disturb the distant Oort Cloud.  If true, each close approach would unleash a million‑year comet shower that could drive extinction‑level impacts on Earth.  Understanding whether such a stellar companion exists reshapes how we assess long‑term planetary risk.

<Callout type="takeaway">If Nemesis exists, its periodic passes could drive comet showers that trigger mass extinctions on Earth.</Callout>

## What happened
The Oort Cloud is a roughly spherical swarm of icy bodies extending out to about 1.6 light‑years from the Sun, containing trillions of comets.  Muller argues that a red dwarf companion on a slightly eccentric, high‑inclination orbit periodically plunges into the densest part of this cloud.  During those passages, billions of comets are nudged onto trajectories that head toward the inner Solar System, creating a comet shower that can last up to a million years.

Muller points to the 1982 study by paleontologists David Raup and Jack Sepkoski, which identified a strong 26‑million‑year periodicity in marine extinction events.  One of the most dramatic peaks coincides with the K‑T boundary 66 million years ago, the event that wiped out the dinosaurs.  The Nemesis hypothesis offers a single, repeatable mechanism that could explain these synchronized extinction spikes.

## Why it matters
If a hidden companion is periodically stirring the Oort Cloud, the risk of Earth‑impacting comets is not random but clustered in time.  This has implications for long‑term planetary defense strategies, which currently focus on near‑Earth asteroids but often overlook distant comet sources.  Moreover, confirming or refuting Nemesis would sharpen our understanding of how stellar dynamics influence planetary habitability, a key question for both Earth’s future and the search for life elsewhere.

## How to think about it
Treat the Nemesis scenario as a low‑probability, high‑impact factor in planetary‑defense planning.  Incorporate periodic comet‑shower windows into long‑term risk assessments, but balance them against more immediate asteroid threats.  Support deep‑infrared sky surveys that can detect faint, cool objects at distances beyond 100 AU, and encourage interdisciplinary studies that link paleontological data with celestial mechanics.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume Nemesis is confirmed; avoid allocating major funding to missions solely aimed at finding it until independent evidence emerges.</Callout>
