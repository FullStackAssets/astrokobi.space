At a recent conference in Munich, Isar Aerospace’s founder Daniel Metzler announced that the German launch firm now has a launch pipeline so full it is fifteen times oversubscribed through 2028. The company, which became the first privately financed European rocket to reach orbit in September, is betting on reusable technology to slash costs and challenge SpaceX’s dominance. With European defense customers shifting to home‑grown providers, the stakes for affordable, sovereign access to space have never been higher.
<Callout type="takeaway">Isar Aerospace’s oversubscribed launch queue and reusable rocket program position it to directly contest SpaceX’s market lead.</Callout>
## What happened
Metzler told reporters that SpaceX, founded in 2002, took four attempts to reach orbit while Isar succeeded in two. He recalled his childhood, saying, "SpaceX was founded in 2002. Back then I was playing with Pokemon cards," and added that within the next 18 years his company could be ahead of SpaceX.

Isar’s order book now shows 14 customers per scheduled rocket, and the firm is 15 times oversubscribed for launches slated for 2028. The mix of demand has flipped from 85 % commercial and 15 % military eighteen months ago to a majority of defense contracts today, reflecting Europe’s push for indigenous launch capability.

The German startup is preparing its first reusable‑rocket test, planning to measure the first‑stage re‑entry on the next flight and to introduce dedicated reusable hardware over the following three to four missions.
## Why it matters
If Isar can deliver reliable, reusable launch services at lower cost, European satellite operators and defense agencies could reduce dependence on U.S. providers and drive down global launch prices. A competitive European launch sector also strengthens strategic autonomy, allowing nations to launch sensitive payloads without foreign approval. However, achieving reusability at scale requires significant engineering breakthroughs and sustained funding, making the timeline uncertain.

## How to think about it
Treat Isar’s trajectory as a case study in emerging‑market disruption: evaluate the strength of its order backlog, monitor progress on reusable‑rocket milestones, and weigh geopolitical benefits against technical risk. Investors should look for clear milestones—successful first‑stage recovery, cost per kilogram benchmarks, and signed defense contracts—before committing significant capital. Policymakers can support the effort through stable procurement contracts and shared test facilities, reducing the financial burden on the startup.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume reusability will be commercially viable within a single launch cycle; the technology still faces unproven engineering challenges that could delay cost reductions.</Callout>
