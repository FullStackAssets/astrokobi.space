On November 5, 2026, Expedition 75 flight engineers Anil Menon and Joshua Kutryk completed the eighth and final iROSA installation on the ISS. The six‑hour‑plus spacewalk saw the 750‑pound solar array unfurl to its full 63‑foot length, boosting the station’s power generation by as much as 30%. The upgrade marks the culmination of a 15‑year effort to replace the aging original panels with modern roll‑out technology, ensuring the station can operate longer and support more experiments.

<Callout type="takeaway">The final iROSA adds up to 30% more power, completing a multi‑year solar upgrade for the ISS.</Callout>

## What happened
The EVA began at 8:36 a.m. EST and lasted 6 hours 59 minutes. Menon and Kutryk retrieved the 750‑pound iROSA from a temporary stowage platform, used Canadarm2 to position it on the starboard side of the 3B power channel, and bolted it into a pre‑installed bracket kit.

During cable connection they observed only half the expected shunt current, prompting Mission Control to pause deployment until the station entered Earth’s shadow. After inspecting the connectors and confirming normal shunt currents on the ground, they received a “go for deploy” from the flight director.

With the issue resolved, the crew released two bolts and the array unfurled autonomously, using stored potential energy in its rolled‑up carbon‑composite booms. The full extension took about ten minutes, completing the iROSA suite that began with the first deployment in 2021.

## Why it matters
The new array increases the ISS’s available electricity by up to 30%, supporting higher‑power experiments, more robust life‑support systems, and future commercial modules. It also demonstrates the reliability of roll‑out solar technology, which could be a baseline for next‑generation habitats on the Moon or Mars. Finally, the successful international teamwork—NASA and CSA astronauts working together—reinforces the collaborative model essential for long‑duration deep‑space missions.

## How to think about it
Treat the iROSA upgrade as a case study in incremental, modular upgrades for long‑duration platforms. Engineers should schedule EVAs to align with orbital lighting conditions, perform thorough ground‑based electrical testing, and design redundancy into power‑distribution pathways. Policy makers can view the project as evidence that international partnerships reduce cost and risk while delivering critical capability upgrades.

<Callout type="warning">Do not attempt roll‑out solar deployments without completing full shunt‑current verification and waiting for a safe shadow window, as premature activation can damage the array.</Callout>
