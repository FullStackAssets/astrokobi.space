On October 19, 2026, Japan’s JAXA will launch the Martian Moons Exploration (MMX) spacecraft aboard an H3 rocket from Tanegashima. The five‑year mission will orbit Mars’s larger moon Phobos, deploy a lander, and return soil and pebble samples to Earth – a first for any Martian satellite. Understanding Phobos’s composition could finally settle the debate over whether the moons are captured asteroids or debris from a giant impact, reshaping our picture of Mars’s history.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX will be the first mission to bring back material from a Martian moon, potentially unlocking the origin of the moons.</Callout>

## What happened
The MMX spacecraft is scheduled to lift off on Monday, October 19 at 20:41 BST from the Tanegashima Space Center on Japan’s H3 launch vehicle. The mission is a collaboration between JAXA, NASA, the French and German space agencies, and the European Space Agency, reflecting a broad international commitment to sample‑return science.

Once in Mars orbit, MMX will perform a series of close fly‑bys before releasing a small lander onto Phobos’s surface. The lander will use a novel touch‑and‑go technique to collect regolith and small pebbles in Phobos’s extremely low gravity—about 0.1 % of Earth’s—and then transfer the material to the orbiter for the return journey.

The orbiter will depart Mars in 2029, cruise back to Earth, and splash down with the sealed sample container. Scientists aim to analyze the material for isotopic signatures and mineralogy that can discriminate between capture‑origin and impact‑origin theories for the Martian moons.

## Why it matters
If Phobos’s composition matches that of Mars, it would support the giant‑impact hypothesis, suggesting the moons formed from debris ejected by a colossal collision. Conversely, a composition similar to carbon‑rich asteroids would bolster the capture scenario, offering a rare glimpse into early solar‑system bodies that were otherwise lost.

Beyond planetary science, MMX serves as a technology demonstrator for low‑gravity sampling, a capability crucial for future asteroid mining, lunar polar resource extraction, and even missions to the icy moons of the outer planets. The mission also reinforces Japan’s growing reputation as a leader in complex sample‑return operations, following the successful Hayabusa and Hayabusa‑2 asteroid missions.

## How to think about it
Treat MMX as a case study in risk‑managed exploration: evaluate the mission’s scientific payoff against its engineering uncertainties, monitor the incremental data releases (e.g., remote sensing of Phobos during cruise), and consider how its sampling tech could be repurposed for commercial ventures. For enthusiasts, follow the scheduled milestones—launch, Phobos landing, Earth return—to gauge progress and anticipate when the first papers will appear.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the returned samples will definitively resolve Phobos’s origin; interpretation will depend on complementary data and may still leave multiple scenarios viable.</Callout>
