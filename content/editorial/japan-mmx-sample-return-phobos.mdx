Japan is set to launch the Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) spacecraft on 19 October 2026, aiming to become the first probe to return material from orbit around Mars. After a ten‑month cruise, MMX will spend roughly three years circling the planet, deploying the IDEFIX rover on Phobos to drill and collect regolith. The mission plans to retrieve the samples, fly by Deimos, and send a return capsule back to Earth for a 2031 landing in Australia. Success would give scientists the first physical pieces of a Martian moon, opening a new window on the origins of the Red Planet’s satellites and the early solar system. The ambitious timeline and international hardware make MMX a landmark in planetary exploration.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX could deliver the first Martian‑moon material to Earth, reshaping our view of solar‑system formation.</Callout>

## What happened
MMX will launch aboard Japan’s H3 rocket from Tanegashima Space Center, separating into a propulsion module, an exploration module, and a return module. The exploration module carries the IDEFIX rover, a Raman Spectrometer (RAX), a miniaturized radiometer (miniRAD), and the MEGANE gamma‑ray and neutron detector, while the return module houses the sealed sample‑return capsule.

Following a ten‑month interplanetary cruise, the spacecraft will enter Mars orbit in early 2027 and begin a three‑year campaign of Phobos observations. In late 2028 the rover will descend to Phobos’ surface, drill up to 10 cm, and store up to 30 g of material in the capsule. After departure, MMX will conduct a series of Deimos fly‑bys before firing its engine for a November 2030 Earth‑bound trajectory, with the capsule slated to splash down in Australia in 2031.

## Why it matters
If successful, MMX will provide the first physical samples from a Martian moon, allowing scientists to test competing theories about whether Phobos and Deimos are captured asteroids or remnants of a giant impact that formed Mars’ moons. The composition of the regolith can also reveal the chemical environment of the early inner solar system, refining models of planetary formation. Moreover, the mission demonstrates international collaboration and advances in autonomous landing, sample handling, and deep‑space return technologies that will underpin future Mars and asteroid sample‑return efforts.

## How to think about it
Track the mission’s milestones—launch, cruise, Mars orbit insertion, Phobos landing, and return capsule release—to gauge progress and potential delays. Consider how the sample analysis will intersect with existing Martian meteorite studies, and what new isotopic signatures might overturn current models. Use MMX as a case study for evaluating the feasibility of future multi‑target sample‑return missions, especially those that combine rover science with rapid return capabilities.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the samples will be commercially available; they will be subject to scientific curation and planetary‑protection protocols.</Callout>
