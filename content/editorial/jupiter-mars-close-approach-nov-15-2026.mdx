On the night of November 15‑16, 2026, Jupiter and Mars will rise together in the constellation Leo, appearing just over 1 degree apart. This visual pairing, known as an appulse, is a perspective trick—while the planets are actually separated by more than 368 million miles, they will look side‑by‑side from Earth. For skywatchers across the United States the planets will become visible around local midnight as Regulus glows below. Because the next similar close approach won’t occur until July 2029, the event offers a fleeting chance to photograph two of the Solar System’s most iconic worlds together.

## What happened
Jupiter and Mars will appear a little over 1 degree apart as they rise over the eastern horizon around midnight for viewers in the U.S., set against the blue‑white light of Regulus in Leo. The alignment is an appulse, a perspective effect that repeats roughly every 2‑2.5 years, but the next comparable separation won’t happen until July 2029. Although the planets are actually separated by about 368 million miles (592 million km), the line‑of‑sight geometry makes them look adjacent.
A 6‑inch telescope can reveal Jupiter’s banded cloud zones and Mars’s reddish disk with dark surface features, offering a stark visual contrast between a gas giant and a rocky world. Even without optics, the two bright points are easy to spot with the naked eye under dark skies.
## Why it matters
The event provides a natural outreach hook, inviting the public to connect with planetary science and the motions of our Solar System. Schools, clubs, and casual observers can use the appulse to spark interest in astronomy, telescope use, and astrophotography. While the alignment carries no new scientific data—its value is purely geometric—it highlights how orbital mechanics create memorable sky events that inspire future explorers.

## How to think about it
Plan your observing site away from city lights and check the weather forecast for clear skies. Use a planet‑position app to pinpoint the exact moment of minimum separation for your latitude, then set up a modest 4‑6‑inch telescope or a camera with a telephoto lens. Allow the eyes to adapt for 10‑15 minutes, and consider a short exposure stack to capture both Jupiter’s cloud bands and Mars’s surface details. Share your images with the community to keep the excitement alive.
