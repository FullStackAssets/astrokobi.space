JWST has revealed the oldest known compact quasar pair, a discovery that upends expectations for the early universe. The system, designated J1602+4228AB and nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, was observed as it appeared 900 million years after the Big Bang. Its two supermassive black holes, each billions of times the Sun’s mass, sit only 18,100 light‑years apart. Finding such massive, tightly bound objects at Cosmic Dawn forces astronomers to rethink how black holes grew so quickly. The find also showcases JWST’s power to probe the universe’s first few hundred million years.

<Callout type="takeaway">The newly found quasar duo proves supermassive black holes existed far earlier than theory predicts.</Callout>

## What happened
Using JWST’s infrared imaging together with follow‑up spectroscopy from Gemini North, researchers identified a compact source at redshift z≈7.2. The pair, J1602+4228AB, appears as a single glowing object whose light has traveled over 13 billion years to reach us.

Detailed analysis shows the two nuclei are separated by roughly 18,100 light‑years—about one‑fifth the Milky Way’s diameter—and host black holes of ~1.5 billion and ~400 million solar masses. This makes Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua the most compact quasar pair ever seen during the Cosmic Dawn epoch.

## Why it matters
The existence of such massive, closely spaced black holes less than a billion years after the Big Bang challenges conventional growth models that rely on steady accretion over long timescales. It suggests either that black‑hole seeds were far larger than previously thought, that accretion rates can far exceed the Eddington limit, or that mergers played a dominant role very early on. These possibilities ripple through theories of galaxy formation, the re‑ionization timeline, and the interpretation of early‑universe observations.

## How to think about it
Treat the discovery as a boundary condition rather than a typical example. Incorporate its properties into simulations as an extreme case, explore alternative seed mechanisms (e.g., direct‑collapse black holes), and evaluate whether brief super‑Eddington phases could produce the observed masses. For observers, prioritize deep, wide‑field JWST surveys and high‑resolution spectroscopy to locate more compact quasar pairs, which will clarify whether Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua is an outlier or part of a hidden population.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume this single system represents the typical population of early quasars; more data are needed before revising broad cosmological models.</Callout>
