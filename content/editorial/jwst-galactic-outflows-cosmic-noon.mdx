The James Webb Space Telescope has captured sweeping streams of gas being expelled from massive galaxies that lived roughly eight to twelve billion years ago, the epoch known as “cosmic noon.” These outflows, driven by supernovae and supermassive black holes, appear far more common and energetic than earlier observations suggested. By directly measuring the cold gas being ejected, astronomers can link the loss of star‑forming material to the rapid decline of stellar birth in those galaxies. Understanding this mechanism reshapes our picture of how the universe’s most productive star‑forming era came to an end.

<Callout type="takeaway">Galactic outflows were the primary engine that quenched star formation at cosmic noon.</Callout>

## What happened
JWST spectroscopy of distant, massive galaxies revealed large‑scale gaseous winds moving at several hundred kilometers per second. The data show that these winds carry enough mass to deplete the reservoirs of cold molecular gas that fuel new stars.

The study attributes the strongest outflows to accretion onto central supermassive black holes, while supernova explosions also contribute. Across the sample, galaxies with the most powerful winds exhibit the lowest current star‑formation rates, indicating a causal relationship.

## Why it matters
If outflows are the dominant quenching mechanism, models of galaxy evolution must treat feedback from black holes and supernovae as the primary regulator of star formation, reshaping predictions of galaxy mass growth and the timeline of cosmic structure formation.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a galaxy’s future star‑forming potential, first assess the presence and strength of outflows via spectral line diagnostics. If winds exceed the galaxy’s gas‑binding energy, treat the system as entering a quenching phase and adjust growth forecasts accordingly. Incorporate both black‑hole accretion rates and supernova rates into feedback prescriptions to capture the full spectrum of outflow drivers.

<Callout type="warning">The exact contribution of supernova‑driven versus black‑hole‑driven winds remains uncertain, so models should treat the balance as a variable rather than a fixed ratio.</Callout>
