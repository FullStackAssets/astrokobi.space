A planet that glows like a mirror has just been spotted circling its star in less than a day. Using JWST’s NIRSpec instrument, astronomers measured phase‑curve variations of the ultra‑short‑period world TOI‑561b and found an unexpectedly high albedo. The discovery suggests the planet retains a reflective atmosphere despite scorching temperatures that should strip gases away. If the atmosphere is secondary, it could be fed by volcanic outgassing from a global magma ocean. The result forces a rethink of atmospheric survival on the hottest rocky exoplanets.

<Callout type="takeaway">TOI‑561b’s bright, likely secondary atmosphere shows that even the most irradiated rocky planets can retain gases.</Callout>

## What happened
Ultra‑short‑period (USP) planets orbit their stars in under 24 hours and are bathed in intense stellar radiation that normally erodes any volatile envelope. TOI‑561b, a 1.5‑Earth‑radius world discovered by TESS, was previously flagged as a possible outlier after a 2025 JWST snapshot hinted at water‑ and carbon‑dioxide‑rich gases. The new study, led by Samuel Boucher and colleagues, obtained continuous NIRSpec phase‑curve data across multiple orbital cycles to map the planet’s day‑night brightness.

The phase‑curve analysis revealed a geometric albedo near 0.6, far higher than the dark basaltic surfaces expected for a lava world. Such reflectivity points to a high‑altitude layer of scattering particles—either silicate clouds lofted from a molten surface or a thin gas envelope enriched in refractory species. The authors argue the atmosphere is secondary, generated by continuous outgassing from a global magma ocean that replenishes material faster than stellar winds can remove it.

## Why it matters
A reflective, possibly secondary atmosphere on a USP challenges the prevailing view that these planets are barren, atmosphere‑free rocks. If volcanic outgassing can sustain a veil of gas under extreme irradiation, the habitability window for close‑in rocky worlds may be broader than assumed. The finding also provides a template for interpreting phase‑curve signals from other ultra‑hot exoplanets, linking surface magma dynamics to observable atmospheric signatures.

## How to think about it
When evaluating USP planets, treat atmospheric detection as a spectrum of possibilities rather than a binary outcome. Combine phase‑curve albedo measurements with emission spectroscopy to separate cloud decks from gas layers, and incorporate interior‑exsolution models that predict volatile release rates. Prioritize targets with bright host stars and short orbital periods, because their frequent transits maximize JWST’s signal‑to‑noise for phase monitoring.

<Callout type="warning">Do not generalize TOI‑561b’s atmospheric properties to all ultra‑short‑period planets without additional observations.</Callout>
