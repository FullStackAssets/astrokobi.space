The ultra‑short‑period planet TOI‑561b circles its star in just 0.4 days, placing its surface at temperatures hot enough to melt rock. Recent JWST/NIRSpec phase‑curve observations captured the planet’s reflected light across multiple orbital phases, revealing an unexpectedly high albedo of roughly 0.7. The research team interprets this brightness as evidence for a silicate‑rich atmosphere, likely sourced from a global magma ocean. If confirmed, the finding reshapes expectations for atmospheric survival on the hottest rocky worlds.

<Callout type="takeaway">TOI‑561b reflects more than two‑thirds of its star’s light, revealing a silicate‑rich, secondary atmosphere on a lava world.</Callout>

## What happened
Ultra‑short‑period (USP) planets are generally thought to be airless because intense stellar radiation strips away volatile envelopes. TOI‑561b, however, stood out in earlier JWST data that hinted at a thin atmosphere containing water‑vapor and CO₂. The new study employed high‑precision NIRSpec phase‑curve measurements, tracking the planet’s brightness as it moved from day to night side. The resulting light curve showed a pronounced reflected‑light component, implying a surface or atmospheric layer that scatters starlight efficiently.

The authors, led by Samuel Boucher, argue that the most plausible source of this reflectivity is a cloud of silicate particles lofted from a molten surface, creating a high‑albedo haze. Their analysis, published in *The Astronomical Journal*, suggests the atmosphere is secondary—generated by outgassing from the magma ocean rather than retained primordial gases.

## Why it matters
A detectable atmosphere on a USP lava world challenges the prevailing view that such planets are barren. It provides a natural laboratory for studying magma‑driven outgassing, a process that may have operated on early Earth and Venus. The result also forces a reevaluation of atmospheric loss models, which must now accommodate rapid replenishment mechanisms. For the broader exoplanet community, the discovery expands the target list for atmospheric characterization, indicating that even the most irradiated rocky worlds can retain observable envelopes.

## How to think about it
When evaluating USP planets, consider three layers of inference: (1) stellar irradiation sets a temperature floor that dictates whether a magma ocean can exist; (2) a magma ocean can supply volatile species that condense into high‑albedo clouds; (3) observational signatures—phase‑curve amplitude, spectral slope, and eclipse depth—must be jointly modeled to separate surface reflection from atmospheric scattering. Applying this framework helps distinguish true atmospheres from bare, reflective lava surfaces.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the measured albedo uniquely identifies silicate clouds; additional spectra at shorter wavelengths are needed to confirm composition.</Callout>
