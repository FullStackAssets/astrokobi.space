Sixty years ago the Leonids lit up the predawn sky over the United States with a record‑breaking storm of 144,000 meteors per hour. That event, part of a series of historic outbursts in 1833 and 1966, remains a benchmark for meteor observers. Modern forecasts for the 2026 Leonid peak, however, predict a much weaker display. Understanding why the 1966 spectacle is unlikely to repeat helps skywatchers set realistic expectations.

<Callout type="takeaway">A Leonid storm of 144,000 meteors per hour is extremely unlikely in 2026.</Callout>

## What happened
The 1966 Leonid storm produced an estimated 144,000 meteors per hour, dwarfing the tens‑of‑thousands per hour recorded in the 1833 outburst. In contrast, more recent returns in 1999, 2001 and 2002 yielded only a few thousand meteors per hour, reflecting the diminishing density of the comet’s dust trails.

Leonid meteors originate from debris left by comet Tempel‑Tuttle, which orbits the Sun every 33⅓ years. Each perihelion passage deposits fresh streams of dust; when Earth intersects a dense, recently ejected trail, a meteor storm can occur. The comet last passed perihelion in 1998, and the 2026 encounter will intersect older, more dispersed material, explaining the forecast of a weak shower.

## Why it matters
A spectacular meteor storm can draw huge public interest, inspire amateur astronomy, and provide valuable data on cometary dust distribution. Conversely, over‑hyped expectations risk disappointment and may divert resources from more scientifically productive observations. Accurate forecasting also helps planners manage light‑pollution concerns and safety for night‑time events.

## How to think about it
Treat the 2026 Leonids as a modest meteor shower rather than a guaranteed storm. Plan observations for the pre‑dawn hours when the radiant is highest, use wide‑field cameras to capture faint meteors, and consult real‑time flux forecasts from the International Meteor Organization. If you travel, prioritize dark‑sky locations but keep expectations realistic; a handful of bright meteors per hour is more likely than a historic outburst.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume a high meteor count; avoid traveling to remote sites solely based on hype.</Callout>
