NASA's Perseverance rover has been carrying tiny patches of spacesuit fabric as it circles Mars. After more than four Earth years—1,645 Martian sols—the material shows barely any visible wear. Researchers compared the Martian dust that settled on the swatches to lunar regolith, which famously scratched visor glass and clogged filters during Apollo missions. The finding that Martian particles are smoother could ease engineering and health concerns for future crewed missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">Martian dust appears gentler on spacesuit material than the abrasive lunar dust that plagued Apollo crews.</Callout>

## What happened

Perseverance carries several swatches of spacesuit material, each less than a square inch (about 6.5 cm²), mounted on its deck for passive exposure to the Martian environment. Over 1,645 sols the swatches collected dust that was later examined for signs of abrasion, compared with control samples and with lunar regolith analogs.

The analysis showed little visible wear on the fabric after the full exposure period. Microscopic inspection revealed that Martian particles are generally rounded, resembling river pebbles, whereas lunar grains are jagged and sharp. This morphology suggests a lower propensity for scratching equipment or irritating human tissue.

## Why it matters

If Martian dust is less abrasive, hardware such as solar panels, cameras, and seals may experience slower degradation, extending mission lifetimes and reducing maintenance demands. For crewed missions, smoother particles could lessen the risk of eye or lung irritation that plagued Apollo astronauts, simplifying suit and habitat filtration requirements. However, the presence of ultrafine particles still demands robust protective measures.

## How to think about it

Treat Martian dust as a lower‑abrasion hazard than lunar regolith, but not as harmless. Design suits and equipment with filtration systems that target sub‑micron particles, and incorporate wear‑resistant coatings where abrasion could still occur. Use the Perseverance data as a baseline, but validate with additional measurements from diverse terrains and during dust‑storm events before finalizing crewed‑mission hardware.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume Martian dust is completely benign; ultrafine particles remain a health and engineering risk, and the current findings are based on a single rover’s limited exposure.</Callout>
