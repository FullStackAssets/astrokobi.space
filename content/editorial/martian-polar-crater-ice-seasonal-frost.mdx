Mars' northern polar region continues to surprise scientists. A new study examined high‑resolution images of 36 craters within the north polar ice cap, tracking bright deposits from 2006 to 2025. Researchers found that 32 craters show clear seasonal brightening and darkening, indicating ice expands in winter and retreats in summer. These findings give a rare glimpse of an active water cycle on a planet long thought to be geologically static.

<Callout type=\"takeaway\">Seasonal frost cycles actively reshape ice within most Martian polar craters.</Callout>

## What happened
The international team analyzed HiRISE images taken over Mars Years 28–38 (2006‑2025) for 36 craters located on the north polar cap. Brightness changes on crater floors were measured as a proxy for surface ice, revealing that 32 craters exhibited a seasonal pattern of brightening in winter and darkening in summer.

Four craters showed no detectable change, which the authors attribute to either a lack of frost accumulation on the crater rims or observational limitations. The study highlights that the seasonal frost coating the crater edges likely contributes to the observed brightness variations.

These results complement existing knowledge of Mars' polar caps, where water‑ice dominates the north cap and carbon‑dioxide ice dominates the south, and where seasonal CO₂ frost can be a meter thick.

## Why it matters
Seasonal ice dynamics at the crater scale provide direct evidence of active water exchange between the atmosphere and surface, refining models of Martian climate evolution. Understanding these cycles improves predictions of past habitability and informs the selection of future landing sites that may access near‑surface water resources. The findings also demonstrate that even modest topographic features can respond rapidly to seasonal forcing, challenging the view of Mars as a largely inert world.

## How to think about it
Treat Martian polar craters as micro‑climates that respond quickly to atmospheric humidity and temperature swings. When evaluating climate scenarios, incorporate seasonal frost deposition and sublimation cycles at the scale of tens of kilometers. For mission planners, prioritize craters that show strong seasonal signals as potential sites for in‑situ water‑ice sampling.

<Callout type=\"warning\">Do not assume that brightness changes directly quantify ice volume; additional radar or in‑situ measurements are needed to confirm thickness and purity.</Callout>
