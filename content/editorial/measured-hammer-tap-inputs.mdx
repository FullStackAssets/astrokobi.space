The latest NASA TB‑26‑09 report dives deep into instrumented hammer‑tap testing, showing that the actual force waveform—whether rippled, separated, or half‑sine—directly impacts the validity of modal analysis. Researchers measured complete force histories with high‑bandwidth sensors, exposing how crest ripples and re‑contact events add spectral content that a simple half‑sine cannot capture. This matters because engineers rely on these tests to certify spacecraft structures before they face the extreme shocks of launch.

<Callout type="takeaway">A hammer‑tap test is only adequate when its measured force history matches the test’s specific objective, not when it merely looks like a half‑sine.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA’s team instrumented a hammer to record the full force‑time profile during modal‑analysis tests. They compared three typical inputs: a classic half‑sine, a rippled contact that maintains force, and a sequence with separated contacts that includes zero‑force intervals. Spectral analysis (Figure 2) showed that the half‑sine, while broadband, contains nulls at odd multiples of the fundamental frequency (f/fo = 3, 5, 7…).

Further, Figure 3 demonstrated that crest rippling fills those nulls, and the timing of separated contacts creates deterministic interference patterns that enrich the excitation spectrum. The report stresses that adequacy hinges on measurement bandwidth, calibration, dynamic range, and signal‑to‑noise ratio, not on waveform shape alone.

## Why it matters
If engineers assume a half‑sine automatically excites all relevant modes, they may miss critical resonances, leading to under‑predicted structural responses during launch. Accurate force measurement ensures that the test covers the frequency, amplitude, and nonlinear ranges required for the spacecraft’s operational envelope. Mis‑characterized tests can propagate errors into finite‑element models, increasing risk and cost during later design phases.

## How to think about it
Treat each hammer‑tap test as a data‑driven experiment: first define the modal‑analysis objectives, then verify that the recorded force history spans the needed frequency band with sufficient signal‑to‑noise. Use the measured spectrum—not the visual shape—to confirm excitation of target modes. If gaps appear, consider adding controlled ripples or adjusting contact timing rather than defaulting to a half‑sine.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume a half‑sine waveform guarantees adequate excitation; always validate the measured spectrum against test objectives.</Callout>
