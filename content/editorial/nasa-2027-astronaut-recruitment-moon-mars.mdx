NASA has announced that applications are now open for its next astronaut class, slated to begin training in 2027. U.S. citizens have until 11:59 p.m. EST on Dec 3 to submit their materials, aiming to join a crew that will operate the upcoming lunar base and eventually travel to Mars. The agency plans to make its final selections by late 2027, building on a 2025 class of ten candidates who are currently in training. This recruitment cycle signals a renewed push toward a sustained lunar presence and the first crewed missions beyond Earth orbit.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s 2027 astronaut class will be the backbone of the Moon Base and the stepping stone to crewed Mars missions.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA opened the application window for its next astronaut class, inviting U.S. citizens to submit a resume, flight hours, education, and personal statements by 11:59 p.m. EST on Dec 3. The selection board will review the pool—expected to be in the thousands—against criteria of technical excellence, teamwork, and adaptability, with final selections announced in late 2027.

The agency’s last recruitment in 2024 yielded ten candidates who are now completing nearly two years of training for lunar surface operations. Historically, NASA has selected 370 individuals since the 1960s, and the current astronaut corps numbers 32 active members.

These new candidates will train for lunar surface activities, long‑duration habitation, and the operational systems that will support the first crewed missions to Mars.

## Why it matters
A fresh cohort of astronauts is essential for NASA’s Artemis‑derived Moon Base, which will serve as a testbed for life‑support, in‑situ resource utilization, and crew autonomy. Success on the Moon directly de‑rises the technical risk of sending humans to Mars, a goal that underpins U.S. leadership in deep‑space exploration. Moreover, the high‑visibility recruitment drives public interest, encouraging a new generation to pursue STEM careers that will sustain the long‑term space economy.

## How to think about it
If you meet the basic eligibility—U.S. citizenship, a bachelor’s degree in a STEM field, and relevant professional experience—start by documenting flight hours, research achievements, and leadership roles. Tailor your personal statement to highlight problem‑solving under pressure, teamwork in high‑stakes environments, and a clear motivation for lunar or Martian exploration. Treat the application as a portfolio: seek feedback from mentors, polish technical writing, and be prepared for a rigorous interview and medical evaluation. Even if you are not selected, the process can clarify career goals and open doors in the broader aerospace sector.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that submitting an application guarantees selection; the process is highly selective and requires years of training and personal sacrifice.</Callout>
