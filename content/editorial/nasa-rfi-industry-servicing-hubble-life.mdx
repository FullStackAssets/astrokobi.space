The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, is still delivering groundbreaking data but its orbit is decaying, pointing to an uncontrolled re‑entry in roughly six years. On Oct. 29, NASA issued a request for information (RFI) inviting industry to propose in‑space servicing concepts that could raise Hubble’s altitude or add new capabilities. The move reflects growing pressure to preserve Hubble’s scientific output while avoiding the expense of a brand‑new flagship. If a viable solution emerges, the observatory could remain a workhorse for years beyond its planned retirement. The announcement also marks a shift under NASA administrator Jared Isaacman, who previously rejected a similar private proposal.

<Callout type="takeaway">Industry proposals could buy Hubble extra years of science without launching a new telescope.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA’s Oct. 29 RFI asks for “in‑space servicing” ideas ranging from orbital boost maneuvers to hardware upgrades that would extend Hubble’s operational life. The agency notes that atmospheric drag is gradually lowering the telescope’s altitude, and a modest boost could buy roughly a decade of additional observing time before uncontrolled re‑entry becomes inevitable.

The request comes after a 2022 private pitch by SpaceX founder‑turned‑NASA administrator Jared Isaacman was turned down over risk concerns. Since becoming administrator in Dec. 2025, Isaacman has urged the agency to explore “every reasonable opportunity” to keep Hubble flying, citing its continued high demand—astronomers request about seven times more observing time than is available each year.

## Why it matters
Hubble’s legacy includes over 23,000 scientific papers and iconic imagery that fuels public interest in astronomy. Extending its service life preserves a continuous data set that underpins long‑term studies of dark energy, galaxy evolution, and exoplanet atmospheres. From a budget perspective, a modest boost or upgrade could be far cheaper than designing, building, and launching a next‑generation flagship, especially if the solution leverages existing commercial spacecraft.

## How to think about it
Treat the Hubble extension as a cost‑benefit case study: first quantify the scientific value of additional observing years, then compare that against the projected cost and risk of a servicing mission. Evaluate candidate technologies (e.g., robotic grapplers, electric propulsion tugs) for compatibility with Hubble’s legacy bus and existing power margins. Finally, map a timeline that aligns the service mission with the next planned major space‑telescope launch to avoid overlap and ensure a smooth transition of capabilities.

<Callout type="warning">Do not attempt a servicing mission without rigorous debris‑mitigation analysis; a failed attachment could create hazardous orbital debris and jeopardize other assets.</Callout>
