NASA recently completed a flight test of its Safe and Precise Landing—Integrated Capabilities Evolution (SPLICE) system aboard the Alta‑X drone at Rogers Dry Lake. The test simulated a lunar descent on Aug. 27, generating high‑resolution terrain maps and autonomously diverting to safer spots when hazards were detected. Successes in communications, telemetry and command‑and‑control pave the way for a full‑scale demonstration at Voyager Technologies later this month. The experiment marks a tangible advance toward fully autonomous landings on the Moon, Mars and other bodies.

<Callout type="takeaway">SPLICE proved that a spacecraft can independently assess and select safe landing sites in real time.</Callout>

## What happened
On Aug 27, NASA’s SPLICE payload, integrated on an Alta‑X drone, performed a simulated lunar descent at Rogers Dry Lake near the Armstrong Flight Research Center. It used lidar and optical sensors to build a digital elevation map and evaluate candidate landing zones.

When the algorithm flagged an unsafe area, the guidance system commanded the drone to shift to an alternate site, demonstrating real‑time hazard avoidance. The flight also validated data handling, telemetry links and command‑and‑control between the payload and the drone.

Earlier test flights in June and July confirmed communications and prepared the system for a full‑scale test at Voyager Technologies in Mojave, scheduled for later this month.

## Why it matters
Autonomous landing reduces reliance on ground operators, allowing missions to react to unexpected terrain and to target scientifically richer sites. The capability shortens mission planning cycles and could enable landers to operate on bodies with limited prior mapping, such as the lunar south pole or Martian lava tubes. Agencies and commercial partners stand to lower costs and risk by embedding this self‑reliant navigation into future landers and rovers.

## How to think about it
When evaluating autonomous landing tech, consider three dimensions: (1) sensor fidelity – can the system resolve hazards at the required scale? (2) decision latency – does the onboard processor make safe choices within the tight time window of descent? (3) integration overhead – how much mass, power and software complexity does the system add to the vehicle? Weigh these against mission goals: high‑risk scientific targets benefit most, while low‑risk, well‑mapped sites may not justify the added complexity.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume SPLICE is ready for crewed lunar landings until it completes an in‑situ test on the Moon or another planetary body.</Callout>
