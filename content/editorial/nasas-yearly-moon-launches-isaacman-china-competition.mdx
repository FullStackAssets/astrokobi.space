NASA’s top civilian, Jared Isaacman, announced a bold cadence for the agency’s Artemis program: at least one heavy‑lift SLS launch every year. The statement came after the successful Artemis II flight in April 2026 and ahead of the planned Artemis III test flight in 2027. Isaacman framed the push as a response to a renewed lunar race with China and as essential to keeping the United States on a path toward a permanent moon presence. He emphasized that frequent launches are the “muscle‑building” the agency needs for deep‑space missions. The plan signals a shift from occasional flagship flights to a more sustained operational tempo.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA aims to launch a big rocket to the Moon at least once a year to stay ahead in the emerging U.S.–China lunar race.</Callout>

## What happened
Isaacman told Space.com that the agency’s confidence has risen sharply after Artemis II demonstrated Orion’s deep‑space capabilities around the Moon. He said the next milestone, Artemis III, will launch in 2027 to low Earth orbit to practice docking Orion with private lunar landers such as SpaceX’s Starship and Blue Origin’s Blue Moon, a rehearsal for future surface missions.

He also highlighted that the United States must match China’s accelerating lunar ambitions, noting that Beijing has publicly outlined plans for a crewed Moon landing. The competition, Isaacman argued, justifies a minimum cadence of one SLS liftoff per year to keep technical expertise sharp and to reassure Congress of the program’s relevance.

## Why it matters
A yearly launch rhythm would transform Artemis from a series of isolated milestones into a continuous operational system, preserving the specialized workforce and supply chain needed for deep‑space exploration. It also serves as a strategic signal to China and other emerging space powers that the U.S. remains committed to lunar leadership. However, sustaining such a cadence demands consistent funding, launch infrastructure upgrades, and risk management for the powerful but complex SLS vehicle.

## How to think about it
Policymakers should treat the yearly launch goal as a benchmark, not a hard contract, linking funding to clear milestones and risk assessments. Industry partners need to align supply‑chain capacities with the cadence, investing in modular components that can be refreshed quickly. For the public, understanding that each launch is a step toward a sustainable lunar presence helps frame the long‑term value beyond any single mission.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the annual launch schedule will be funded automatically; congressional approval and realistic budgeting are essential.</Callout>
