As November unfolds, the night sky over the Northern Hemisphere offers a packed schedule of planetary pairings, meteor activity, and moonlit highlights. From a bright Jupiter‑Mars conjunction on the 17th to a series of moon‑planet close approaches later in the month, the celestial show is both visually striking and scientifically useful. Knowing when and where to look lets casual observers capture rare moments and educators tie current events to classroom lessons.
<Callout type="takeaway">The November 2026 sky provides multiple easy‑to‑spot planetary events that deepen public engagement with astronomy.</Callout>
## What happened
On the morning of November 17, Jupiter and Mars appear together in the pre‑dawn sky, creating a striking conjunction that is visible to the naked eye from mid‑latitude northern locations. The same night the Leonid meteor shower reaches its peak, offering a chance to see dozens of meteors per hour under dark conditions.
Earlier in the month Mercury reaches its greatest elongation from the morning Sun, making it the most observable of the inner planets. On the evenings of November 19 and 20 the Moon passes close to Saturn, while the supermoon of November 23‑24 hangs near the bright stars Aldebaran and the Pleiades, creating a vivid foreground.
Later in the month the Moon continues its dance: November 25 it aligns with a trio of bright stars, November 26‑27 it skirts the twin stars of Gemini, and on November 29‑30 it draws near Jupiter, Mars, and the bright star Regulus, giving multiple opportunities for low‑magnification telescopic or binocular viewing.
## Why it matters
These events are not just pretty pictures; they provide tangible reference points for teaching orbital mechanics, planetary motion, and the influence of Earth's atmosphere on visibility. Amateur astronomers can practice tracking fast‑moving objects, while educators can link real‑time observations to curriculum topics such as conjunctions, elongations, and meteor shower dynamics. Public interest spikes during such windows, supporting dark‑sky advocacy and encouraging the preservation of low‑light‑pollution sites.

## How to think about it
Treat the month as a series of observation “modules.” First, schedule a pre‑dawn session on the 17th for the Jupiter‑Mars conjunction and set a reminder for the Leonids later that night. Next, use a planetarium app (e.g., Stellarium) to plot Mercury’s elongation and the Moon’s path near Saturn, the supermoon, and the Gemini twins. Check local weather forecasts each day and prioritize dark‑sky sites for the meteor shower. Finally, record what you see—photos, sketches, or simple notes—to build a personal log that can be shared with clubs or online communities.
<Callout type="warning">Avoid looking at the Sun directly when trying to locate Mercury; use proper solar filters or wait until the planet is well above the horizon in the morning sky.</Callout>
## FAQ

  <Question q="How can I locate the Leonid meteor shower?">Find the constellation Leo and look for meteors radiating from a point near its head; the shower peaks after midnight on November 17, best viewed from a dark site with the sky clear to the north.</Question>
  <Question q="What tools help me track the Moon’s proximity to planets?">Free planetarium software like Stellarium or mobile apps such as SkySafari let you input your location and display real‑time overlays of the Moon’s path relative to planets and bright stars.</Question>
