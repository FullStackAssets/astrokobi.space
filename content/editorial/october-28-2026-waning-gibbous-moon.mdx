Tonight, October 28, 2026, the Moon will appear as a bright waning gibbous, with roughly 94 % of its disk illuminated. NASA’s Daily Moon Guide confirms the phase, placing the Moon just before the third quarter. The high illumination makes several prominent lunar features visible to the naked eye and through modest optics. For amateur astronomers this offers a chance to locate classic craters such as Tycho and Copernicus without a telescope. The timing also signals the approach of the next full Moon on November 24, marking a key point in the 29.5‑day lunar cycle.

## What happened
NASA’s Daily Moon Guide tracker reports that the Moon is in a waning gibbous phase with 94% of its surface illuminated on Wednesday, Oct. 28. This places the Moon just before the third‑quarter stage of its 29.5‑day orbit.

Under these conditions the naked eye can pick out the Tycho crater, Copernicus crater, and the Mare Imbrium. Binocular users will also see Posidonius crater, the Mare Humorum, and the Grimaldi Basin, while a modest telescope reveals the Apollo 12 landing site, the Rupes Altai scarp, and the Caucasus Mountains.

The lunar cycle repeats every 29.5 days, and the next full Moon is scheduled for November 24, giving observers a clear marker for the upcoming phase transition.

## Why it matters
A highly illuminated Moon serves as an accessible teaching tool, allowing beginners to practice locating and identifying lunar landmarks without expensive equipment. The bright surface also aids in calibrating telescopes and camera focus for lunar photography. Conversely, the same brightness raises skyglow, which can hinder observations of faint deep‑sky objects and affect astrophotography planning.

## How to think about it
Start by checking local weather and moonrise times; a clear eastern horizon will give the best view. Locate the bright Tycho crater first, then sweep eastward to find Copernicus and the Mare Imbrium. If you have binoculars, add Posidonius and the Mare Humorum to your checklist. For telescope users, aim for the Apollo 12 site and the rugged Rupes Altai. Photographers should schedule exposures after the Moon has risen high to minimize atmospheric distortion, and consider using a neutral‑density filter if capturing the lunar surface at high detail.

<Callout type="warning">Avoid planning deep‑sky imaging on this night, as the bright moon will significantly increase skyglow.</Callout>
