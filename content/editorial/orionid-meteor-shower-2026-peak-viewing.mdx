The Orionid meteor shower, debris from Halley’s Comet, will reach its peak this week. According to the American Meteor Society, observers can see up to 20 meteors per hour under ideal conditions. The peak occurs Wednesday night into Thursday morning, but a bright waning Moon will linger until after midnight, reducing visibility. The shower is visible from both hemispheres after midnight, spreading across the sky rather than just the Orion constellation. Knowing when and how to watch turns a fleeting sky show into a reliable night‑time event.

<Callout type="takeaway">Peak Orionid activity offers up to 20 meteors per hour, but the Moon’s glow means the best views come after it sets.</Callout>

## What happened
The Orionids are one of two major meteor showers generated by debris left by Halley’s Comet, which passes near Earth roughly every 75 years. During the peak Wednesday night into Thursday morning, the American Meteor Society expects up to 20 meteors per hour in dark, clear skies, and the stream remains visible for several weeks before and after the peak.

The shower can be seen from both the Northern and Southern hemispheres after midnight, with meteors streaking across the entire sky rather than being confined to the Orion constellation. However, a bright waning Moon will remain above the horizon until late, dimming the display until it sets. Light‑polluted locations will further reduce visibility, so a dark site far from city lights is essential.

## Why it matters
For amateur astronomers, the Orionids provide a rare chance to observe fast, bright meteors that can leave glowing trains lasting several seconds, linking today’s skywatchers to the historic legacy of Halley’s Comet. The shower also highlights how Earth’s orbit intersects cometary debris, a natural reminder of the dynamic environment of our solar system. Clear viewing conditions can inspire public interest in astronomy and reinforce the value of preserving dark‑sky sites.

## How to think about it
Choose a dark location well away from streetlights and give your eyes 15‑20 minutes to adapt to the darkness. Check the Moon’s set time for your latitude and plan to observe after it disappears, typically in the early predawn hours. Bring a comfortable chair or blanket, avoid using bright screens, and keep a wide‑angle view of the sky rather than focusing on Orion alone. Simple star‑mapping apps can help you locate the radiant and track meteor activity in real time.

<Callout type="warning">Do not stare at bright artificial lights or drive while scanning the sky; give your eyes time to adjust and stay safe on the ground.</Callout>
