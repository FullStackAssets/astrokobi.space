On November 25, 2026, China will fire a Long March 2F rocket carrying the Shenzhou‑24 crew to its Tiangong space station. The mission, the nation’s 18th crewed flight, will include a Pakistani astronaut serving as a payload specialist—the first foreigner ever to visit Tiangong. The astronaut will spend about a week aboard the station before returning to Earth on Shenzhou‑23. This joint flight follows a bilateral agreement signed in February 2025 that opened the door for Pakistani candidates to train with the China Manned Space Agency. The historic step reshapes the landscape of human spaceflight partnerships.

<Callout type="takeaway">Tiangong will host its first foreign crew member, marking a new era of Chinese‑led international space cooperation.</Callout>

## What happened
China’s Shenzhou‑24 launch is scheduled for November 25, 2026 atop a Long March 2F from the Jiuquan launch site. The three‑person crew will dock with the Tianhe core module of Tiangong, joining the station’s ongoing scientific program. One seat is reserved for a Pakistani astronaut, selected from Muhammad Zeeshan Ali and Khurram Daud, both nominated by SUPARCO.

The China Manned Space Agency began the partnership with Pakistan in February 2025, signing an agreement that committed to training and flight opportunities. After a multi‑stage selection process that concluded in April 2026, the two candidates completed training in China, with one designated as the primary payload specialist and the other as backup. The chosen astronaut will travel to the station as a payload specialist and return on the Shenzhou‑23 vehicle.

The mission also underscores China’s broader diplomatic push. By welcoming a foreign astronaut, Beijing signals a willingness to emulate the collaborative model of the ISS, while Pakistan gains a milestone that puts it ahead of India’s Gaganyaan crewed launch slated for early 2027.

## Why it matters
The flight expands China’s soft power, offering a tangible example of scientific cooperation beyond its borders. For Pakistan, the opportunity accelerates its human‑spaceflight ambitions and provides valuable experience for future missions. The presence of a non‑Chinese crew member may also attract additional international research partnerships, diversifying the experiments conducted aboard Tiangong.

Regional dynamics shift as Pakistan edges ahead of India in crewed spaceflight, potentially influencing South Asian space policy and funding. At the same time, the United States’ Wolf Amendment continues to limit Chinese participation in the ISS, making Tiangong an alternative venue for multinational research.

## How to think about it
Treat the mission as a pilot case for future multinational crews on non‑ISS platforms. Track how the partnership influences subsequent agreements, research proposals, and funding allocations in both China and Pakistan. For policymakers, weigh the scientific benefits against the diplomatic signals sent to rival space powers.
