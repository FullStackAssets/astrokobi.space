Astronomers have identified the source of a mysterious radio signal as a binary system 5,000 light‑years away, composed of a rapidly spinning white dwarf and a sun‑like companion. The system, catalogued as GPM J071507−114153, emits radio pulses every eight minutes, a behavior previously seen only in binaries with cooler red dwarfs. By tracking the pulses, researchers directly observed the white dwarf’s motion and even caught stellar flares on the companion star. This discovery offers a rare glimpse into the mechanisms behind long‑period transients and expands the types of stars known to generate coherent radio bursts.

<Callout type="takeaway">The eight‑minute radio pulses come from the white dwarf, confirming it as the engine of this long‑period transient.</Callout>

## What happened

The team used the Murchison Widefield Array to scan the Milky Way and flagged a transient source that switched on and off on minute‑scale intervals. Follow‑up observations with the Square Kilometre Array Observatory revealed a binary designated GPM J071507−114153 located roughly 5,000 light‑years from Earth. The white dwarf spins once every eight minutes and produces bright, coherent radio bursts that classify the system as a long‑period transient, a class with only about a dozen members discovered since 2021.

High‑resolution timing showed the pulses originate near the white dwarf, allowing the researchers to see the compact star move in its orbit. As team leader Sam McSweeney noted, "This is the first time the pulses are clear enough to see the white dwarf moving in its orbit around its companion." The data also captured flares erupting on the sun‑like star, and a passage of the white dwarf behind a strong magnetic region, described by Natasha Hurley‑Walker as "a stellar prominence… It's amazing that by using radio wavelengths, we can detect the storms on a star over 5,000 light‑years away."

## Why it matters

The detection ties radio transients directly to white dwarfs, resolving a long‑standing ambiguity about which star in similar binaries produces the bursts. Understanding the emission mechanism informs models of magnetic interaction, stellar wind coupling, and plasma physics in compact binaries. It also broadens the search criteria for future radio surveys, suggesting that white‑dwarf systems can be prolific sources of detectable signals, which may influence strategies in both astrophysics and the broader search for technosignatures.

## How to think about it

Treat this discovery as a case study in how multi‑wavelength timing can disentangle complex binary interactions. When evaluating new transients, first verify the orbital dynamics to locate the emission source, then compare the pulse cadence with known classes. Use the presence of accompanying flares or magnetic signatures as clues to the underlying physics rather than jumping to exotic explanations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that similar radio bursts are evidence of extraterrestrial technology without corroborating spectral and polarization data.</Callout>
