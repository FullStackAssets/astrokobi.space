A Senate Commerce Committee hearing on Nov. 10 put NASA’s chief financial officer and inspector general nominees under the spotlight. The committee considered Joshua Shoemaker for CFO and Robert Steinau for IG, two of the four Senate‑confirmed positions at the agency. Both nominees brought decades of experience in government finance and oversight, but received limited questioning. The outcomes matter because NASA’s budget and integrity are central to ambitious programs like the lunar return and commercial space stations.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s new financial and oversight leaders will shape how the agency balances ambitious missions with fiscal responsibility.</Callout>

## What happened
The Senate Commerce Committee held a hearing on Nov. 10 to consider the nominations of Joshua Shoemaker as NASA’s chief financial officer and Robert Steinau as inspector general. Shoemaker, an accountant with more than 20 years in the private sector and Mississippi state government, has been a senior adviser in NASA’s Mission Support Directorate since August and previously served as controller for the Republican National Committee. Steinau has worked at NASA’s Office of Inspector General since 2009 and has been the senior official there since December 2024, following a stint at the Department of Energy’s OIG and the U.S. Secret Service.

In his opening statement, Shoemaker said, "I believe the CFO has an important responsibility to give the administrator, NASA leadership and Congress an honest and timely picture of the agency’s financial position," emphasizing budget visibility, program performance, and modernization of financial systems. Steinau outlined three critical challenges for his office: returning humans to the moon, transitioning from the International Space Station to commercial stations, and strengthening cybersecurity.

The hearing lasted a little more than an hour, with most senators focusing on the FCC nomination of Danielle Thumann rather than probing the NASA nominees in depth.

## Why it matters
NASA’s multi‑billion‑dollar budget funds lunar Artemis missions, deep‑space exploration, and an emerging commercial low‑Earth‑orbit economy. Effective financial oversight ensures that funds are allocated efficiently, reducing the risk of cost overruns that have plagued past programs. Likewise, a vigilant inspector general can safeguard against fraud, waste, and cyber threats that could jeopardize mission success and public trust. The leadership choices therefore influence how quickly and safely NASA can achieve its long‑range goals.

## How to think about it
When evaluating NASA’s leadership, weigh professional expertise against the depth of Senate scrutiny. Consider how each nominee’s prior experience aligns with the agency’s current priorities—budget reform for Shoemaker and mission‑critical oversight for Steinau. Track their early actions, such as proposed reporting changes or audit focus areas, to gauge whether they will reinforce fiscal discipline without stifling innovation.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the nominees will automatically resolve existing budget or oversight challenges; effective change requires sustained congressional support and agency cooperation.</Callout>
