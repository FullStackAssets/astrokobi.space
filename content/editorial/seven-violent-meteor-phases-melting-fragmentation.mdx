When a rock from space streaks across the sky, we picture a brief flash that simply vaporizes. A new analysis of 75 recent fireball recordings, however, shows the descent is a far more violent, multi‑stage process. Researchers identified seven distinct atmospheric phases that govern how meteoroids melt, break apart, and finally reach the ground. Understanding these steps reshapes predictions of where meteorites land and how much material survives.

<Callout type="takeaway">Melting and fragmentation, not just heating, dominate a meteoroid’s journey through Earth’s atmosphere.</Callout>

## What happened
The team examined video and photographic records of 75 meteorite falls, extracting brightness curves, rotation signatures, and fragmentation events. By aligning these observations, they defined seven sequential phases that describe the physical transformations a space rock undergoes from the upper atmosphere to the surface.

In Phase 1, a shock wave forms as the object encounters enough air density to compress the gas ahead of it, producing an initial faint glow. Phase 2 sees the brightness intensify; rapid spin rates—ranging from 0.5 to 5 seconds per rotation—modulate the light output. By Phase 3 the meteor becomes a fireball, and melting overtakes ablation as the primary mass‑loss mechanism, with molten material stripped away by the high‑speed airflow.

## Why it matters
The revised picture alters how scientists estimate surviving mass, landing zones, and impact hazards. Melting and breakup control both the deceleration profile and the dispersion of fragments, which improves recovery efforts and informs planetary‑defense simulations that must account for fragment clouds rather than a single intact body.

## How to think about it
Treat a meteoroid’s descent as a staged process rather than a single heating event. Observe the early shock‑wave flash to gauge entry speed, watch for rapid brightness fluctuations that signal spin, and note any sudden fragmentation bursts that often precede the melting phase. For researchers, incorporating phase‑specific drag and heat‑transfer coefficients into simulations will yield more realistic survivability estimates.

<Callout type="warning">Do not approach freshly fallen fragments until local authorities confirm that the area is safe.</Callout>
