Space debris is no longer a distant nuisance; it is a growing threat that could jeopardize the entire low‑Earth‑orbit economy. A new paper by NASA JPL researcher Slava Turyshev, published in Acta Astronautica, argues that counting objects is insufficient without mechanisms for control and economic responsibility. The study highlights that Starlink satellites performed 144,404 collision‑avoidance maneuvers between December 2024 and May 2025, illustrating how congested the 500‑600 km band has become. Turyshev warns that objects at higher altitudes, around 850 km, lack atmospheric drag and pose a long‑term cascade risk.
<Callout type="takeaway">Effective space‑junk mitigation requires coordinated orbital control and market‑based incentives, not just debris counts.</Callout>
## What happened
Turyshev’s preprint separates low‑Earth‑orbit debris (500‑600 km) from higher‑altitude objects (≈850 km). In the lower band the thin atmosphere still causes objects to deorbit within a few years, providing a natural self‑cleaning effect. By contrast, the higher band contains spent rocket stages and defunct satellites that experience negligible drag, allowing them to remain for decades or centuries and to collide without any built‑in avoidance capability.
The paper also quantifies operational congestion: FCC filings show that SpaceX’s Starlink constellation executed 144,404 collision‑avoidance maneuvers over a six‑month period, roughly one maneuver per satellite each week. This frequency signals that even in the self‑cleaning zone, traffic density is approaching a critical threshold where manual avoidance may become unsustainable.
## Why it matters
If uncontrolled objects at 850 km collide, they can generate a cascade of debris that spreads both upward and downward, threatening active satellites, scientific missions, and future commercial ventures. The economic cost of lost services, insurance premiums, and required maneuver fuel could run into billions, undermining the business case for new constellations. Moreover, without clear liability and incentive structures, operators lack motivation to de‑orbit or actively manage legacy hardware, leaving the problem to accumulate.

## How to think about it
Treat orbital debris as a market externality. First, classify objects by altitude to assign different regulatory regimes. Second, impose liability fees proportional to an object’s projected lifetime in orbit, creating a financial incentive to design for rapid de‑orbit. Third, fund active removal services through a pooled industry levy that rewards proven cleanup technologies. Finally, integrate real‑time collision‑avoidance data into a shared traffic‑management system to reduce the need for ad‑hoc maneuvers.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that voluntary best‑practice guidelines will alone curb high‑altitude debris; without enforceable liability, operators may continue to launch long‑lived stages without mitigation plans.</Callout>
