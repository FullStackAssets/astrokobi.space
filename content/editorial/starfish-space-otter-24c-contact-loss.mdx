Starfish Space’s first Otter servicing satellite, Otter 24C, went silent in low‑Earth orbit just weeks after its debut. The 739‑pound craft, launched on SpaceX’s Transporter‑18 rideshare on Oct 1, was meant to demonstrate debris‑removal and inspection capabilities for NASA’s SSPICY program. A fault in the Astro Digital bus turned off the radio, and the company, NASA, and Astro Digital have declared the satellite unrecoverable. The loss ends the first in‑space test of a commercial servicing platform and raises questions about the readiness of satellite‑maintenance technology.

<Callout type="takeaway">The premature loss of Otter 24C halts NASA’s first in‑orbit servicing demo and underscores the fragility of emerging satellite‑repair tech.</Callout>

## What happened
Otter 24C lifted off on Oct 1 aboard SpaceX’s Transporter‑18 mission, one of 130 payloads delivering a mix of research and commercial satellites to low‑Earth orbit. The 335‑kilogram spacecraft was the inaugural flight for Starfish Space’s Otter line and was tasked with the Small Spacecraft Propulsion and Inspection Capability (SSPICY) program, which aimed to inspect up to four inoperable U.S. space objects over a two‑year period.

During commissioning, Starfish Space stopped receiving telemetry. An investigation traced the outage to an error in the fault‑protection software supplied by Astro Digital, which inadvertently disabled the radio subsystem. With no way to re‑establish contact, Starfish, NASA, and Astro Digital concluded that Otter 24C is not recoverable.

## Why it matters
The failure highlights the technical hurdles of autonomous on‑orbit servicing, a capability that could mitigate the growing debris problem and extend satellite lifespans. A successful demo would have validated hardware, software, and operational concepts for future commercial and government missions. Its loss delays those timelines, potentially slowing the emergence of a market for satellite repair, refueling, and de‑orbiting services, and may prompt more stringent verification of fault‑tolerance in future designs.

## How to think about it
Treat the Otter 24C loss as a case study in risk management for emerging space services. Evaluate any future servicing contract by asking: (1) How redundant are the communication and power subsystems? (2) What independent verification exists for fault‑protection software? (3) Does the provider have a clear contingency plan for loss of telemetry? Applying this checklist can help investors, agencies, and operators gauge maturity before committing to operational missions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a single successful demo guarantees reliability; rigorous testing and redundancy are essential before scaling satellite‑servicing operations.</Callout>
