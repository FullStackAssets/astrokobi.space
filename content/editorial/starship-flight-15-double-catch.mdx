SpaceX is set to lift off Starship Flight 15 on Nov 13, marking the first attempt to recover both stages of an orbital launch by catching them on the launch tower’s “chopstick” arms. The 75‑minute launch window opens at 5:45 p.m. ET from Starbase in Texas, and the mission will last more than 22 hours if everything proceeds as planned. Success would demonstrate a fully reusable launch system, potentially slashing turnaround time and cost for future missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">If successful, Starship will achieve the first complete tower‑catch of both booster and spacecraft.</Callout>

## What happened
Starship Flight 15 is the 15th test flight of the vehicle and is scheduled to launch during a 75‑minute window that opens at 5:45 p.m. ET (2245 GMT) from SpaceX’s Starbase site in South Texas. The mission plans to return both the Super Heavy booster and the Starship upper stage to the launch mount, where they would be caught by the tower’s “chopstick” arms – a maneuver never attempted before.

Super Heavy is expected to touch down on the launch pad just 6.5 minutes after liftoff, while Starship will continue to low‑Earth orbit, deploy 26 next‑gen Starlink V3 satellites, and remain aloft for roughly 22.5 hours before attempting its own tower catch on the afternoon of Nov 14.

## Why it matters
A full tower‑catch would prove that an orbital launch system can be turned around in days rather than weeks, dramatically lowering launch costs and increasing flight cadence. Faster inspection and refurbishment could accelerate the deployment of large satellite constellations, lunar lander components, and eventually crewed missions to the Moon and Mars. The ability to reuse both stages in a single launch also reduces the mass and complexity of ground‑based recovery infrastructure.

## How to think about it
Treat the double‑catch as a new metric in evaluating launch system efficiency: consider turnaround time, cost per kilogram, and risk profile together. If the turnaround time drops below a week, the cost advantage may outweigh the added operational risk. Conversely, if the probability of a tower‑catch failure remains high, traditional pad‑landing may stay preferable for high‑value payloads. Decision‑makers should model both scenarios and factor in schedule flexibility when planning missions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the launch schedule will remain unchanged; technical issues can shift the window, and a failed tower catch could ground Starbase for weeks.</Callout>
