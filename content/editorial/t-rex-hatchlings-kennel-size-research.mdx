Scientists have discovered that the hatchlings of the iconic Tyrannosaurus rex were as small as domestic kittens, a finding that reshapes our understanding of their early life. Using synchrotron imaging at the University of Saskatchewan, researchers measured fossilized bones and inferred a juvenile length of roughly 0.5–1.0 meters. The study, published in Biology on July 7 2026, shows that adult T. rex could reach 12 m and 7,000 kg, while the mothers laid 30–100 eggs per clutch. These tiny hatchlings were largely left to fend for themselves, suggesting a r‑selection reproductive strategy. The implications touch on predator‑prey dynamics and dinosaur life history.

<Callout type="takeaway">T. rex hatchlings were kitten‑sized, implying a high‑mortality, r‑selected reproductive strategy.</Callout>

## What happened

The research team employed the Canadian Light Source synchrotron to scan fossilized juvenile bones, revealing internal structures such as blood vessels and growth lines that are invisible with conventional imaging. Analysis of bone density and micro‑architecture allowed the scientists to estimate the hatchlings’ length at about 0.5–1.0 m, comparable to a domestic cat.

The data confirm that adult T. rex could span up to 40 feet (12 m) and weigh up to 15,000 pounds (7,000 kg). Mother T. rex laid clutches of 30–100 eggs, a strategy consistent with r‑selection where many offspring are produced but few survive to adulthood.

The study also suggests that T. rex may have provided limited parental care—more than some reptiles but less than birds—allowing hatchlings to be vulnerable to predation.

## Why it matters

Understanding the size and survival strategy of T. rex hatchlings informs models of Late Cretaceous ecosystems, predator‑prey interactions, and the evolution of parental investment in large theropods. It also illustrates how modern imaging techniques can unlock new insights from fossil material.

## How to think about it

When evaluating paleontological claims, consider the sample size, the preservation state of specimens, and the assumptions built into size estimations. Compare findings with related theropods to gauge consistency, and remain cautious about extending conclusions beyond the data set.
