The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) has catalogued more than 8,100 candidate exoplanets and confirmed just over 1,000 of them. Among those is the hot Jupiter TOI-2535b, located roughly 1,000 light‑years from Earth, which displayed a regular series of transits for seven years before the signal vanished. Researchers attribute the disappearance to a subtle tilt in the planet’s orbital plane caused by gravitational interaction with a close neighboring planet. This case demonstrates that even modest dynamical nudges can render a planet invisible to the transit method, reshaping how we interpret exoplanet surveys.

<Callout type="takeaway">A planet’s transits can disappear when a nearby companion alters its orbital inclination enough to break the line‑of‑sight alignment.</Callout>

## What happened
TESS observed multiple, consistent transits of TOI-2535b over a seven‑year span, establishing a reliable orbital period and depth. In the eighth year, the expected dip in stellar brightness was absent, prompting a re‑examination of the light curve. The team, led by Samuel Yee of UCLA, analyzed the timing and shape of earlier transits and modeled the system’s dynamics, finding that a neighboring planet induced a gradual inclination change.

The authors explain that a planet is only seen to transit when its orbital normal lies within a few degrees of the sky plane; even a small shift can erase the transit signature. Their simulations show that the companion’s perturbations are sufficient to move TOI-2535b’s orbit out of the narrow geometric window required for detection, effectively making the planet “vanish” from our viewpoint.

## Why it matters
The episode highlights a detection bias inherent to the transit technique: planets that temporarily lose alignment may be missed entirely, skewing occurrence rates for certain orbital configurations. It also underscores the importance of long‑baseline observations and archival data mining, as previously dismissed gaps could conceal dynamic behavior. Finally, understanding such inclination swings informs models of planetary system evolution, especially in tightly packed systems where mutual gravitation is significant.

## How to think about it
When evaluating transit data, treat a missing or intermittent signal as a potential dynamical effect rather than an outright non‑detection. Cross‑check with radial‑velocity measurements to detect non‑transiting companions that could be driving inclination changes. Incorporate long‑term monitoring into survey designs, and revisit archival light curves with updated algorithms that can flag sudden disappearance events.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume a single vanished transit indicates the planet has been destroyed; orbital geometry alone can explain the loss of signal.</Callout>
