The ULA Vulcan Centaur launch on Sunday, Nov. 15, ended in a failure that cost 40 Amazon Leo satellites. ULA has traced the anomaly to an inadequate fuel supply to the two BE‑4 engines on the first‑stage booster, not to engine performance. The finding is significant because it isolates the problem to the fuel system rather than the engines themselves, which are also used on Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket.

<Callout type="takeaway">
The failure was caused by an inadequate fuel supply to the first‑stage BE‑4 engines, not by engine performance.
</Callout>

## What happened
The launch, the fifth ever for Vulcan, was the only one to fail after three of five flights had issues. ULA’s preliminary investigation points to a low booster thrust caused by insufficient fuel delivery to the BE‑4 engines. The vehicle’s six solid rocket boosters and the Centaur V upper stage, which uses RL‑10 engines, performed nominally.

## Why it matters
A root‑cause identification is critical for the reliability of ULA’s new heavy‑lift platform and for the commercial satellite market that relies on it. The loss of 40 satellites represents a significant financial hit for Amazon and underscores the importance of robust fuel‑system design. The incident also affects Blue Origin, whose BE‑4 engines are shared across both companies’ rockets.

## How to think about it
When evaluating launch reliability, focus on the entire fuel delivery chain: propellant storage, transfer lines, and pressure regulation. Use a risk‑mitigation framework that includes design reviews, ground testing of critical interfaces, and iterative flight data analysis. A transparent investigation process builds stakeholder confidence and helps prevent costly setbacks.
