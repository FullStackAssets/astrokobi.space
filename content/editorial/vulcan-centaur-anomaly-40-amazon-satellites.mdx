On November 15, 2026, United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan Centaur lifted off from Cape Canaveral carrying 40 Amazon broadband satellites. Ten minutes into flight, telemetry indicated an anomaly that halted the vehicle, and the Centaur upper stage failed to reach orbit. The loss removes an entire segment of Amazon’s low‑Earth‑orbit internet constellation. The incident also marks the first total mission failure for the Vulcan program after a series of booster‑related issues. Stakeholders from commercial operators to national‑security customers now face renewed uncertainty about ULA’s heavy‑lift reliability.

## What happened
The Vulcan Centaur launched at 12:40 p.m. ET (1740 GMT) from Space Launch Complex 41, tasked with delivering Amazon’s Leo broadband constellation to low Earth orbit. The vehicle’s solid rocket boosters performed nominally, a notable improvement after previous nozzle‑burn‑through incidents in 2024 and early 2026.

Approximately ten minutes after liftoff, ULA’s launch director reported an anomaly. Subsequent statements indicated that the Centaur upper stage malfunctioned, preventing orbital insertion and resulting in the loss of all 40 satellites. This marks the first complete mission loss for Vulcan, contrasting with earlier flights where booster issues were mitigated and payloads reached orbit.

## Why it matters
The failure removes a critical batch of satellites needed for Amazon’s plan to provide global broadband, delaying service rollouts and increasing costs for the company. For ULA, the incident jeopardizes confidence among both commercial and national‑security customers, potentially opening opportunities for competitors like SpaceX. Additionally, the setback could affect the timeline for Vulcan’s human‑rating efforts, which are tied to future crewed missions and NASA contracts.

## How to think about it
Treat the incident as a reminder that even mature launch systems can suffer catastrophic failures. For investors, diversify exposure across multiple launch providers rather than relying on a single vehicle. Policymakers should maintain a robust, competitive launch ecosystem to avoid single‑point failures. Engineers can use the anomaly data to prioritize redundancy in upper‑stage systems and enhance real‑time telemetry analysis.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the next Vulcan flight will be problem‑free; rigorous testing and independent verification remain essential before committing critical payloads.</Callout>
