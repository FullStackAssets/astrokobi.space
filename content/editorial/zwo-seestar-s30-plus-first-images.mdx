ZWO unveiled its Seestar S30 Plus this week, and the first images are already turning heads in the amateur astronomy community. The compact, 30‑mm aperture telescope packs two 3.7‑megapixel cameras and four‑element apochromatic optics, delivering 1,440 × 2,560‑pixel views of nebulae, galaxies and even the Milky Way. Because it weighs just 1.65 kg and ships ready‑to‑use, the device promises to lower the barrier to deep‑sky imaging for beginners. Space.com’s hands‑on test shows the results rival more expensive smart scopes, suggesting a shift toward affordable, plug‑and‑play astrophotography.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Seestar S30 Plus proves high‑quality deep‑sky imaging is possible in a sub‑2‑kg smart telescope.</Callout>

## What happened
ZWO announced the Seestar S30 Plus on November 10, 2026, making it available through the company’s website and Amazon just in time for the holiday season. The new model retains the original 30 mm aperture but upgrades to four‑element apochromatic optics with extra‑low‑dispersion glass and adds two 3.7‑megapixel cameras that each produce 1,440 × 2,560‑pixel images. A wide‑angle 43.5° camera captures Milky Way panoramas, star trails and nightscapes, while built‑in dual‑band filters help suppress light‑pollution glow.

During a month‑long trial the author captured the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), the Pleiades (M45), the Dumbbell Nebula (M27) and the North America Nebula (NGC 7000) with striking clarity. The telescope’s magnetic solar filter also enabled safe solar photography, a rare feature at this price point.

At a retail price well below the $1,300 Draco and far under the $5,000 Vaonis Atlas, the S30 Plus positions itself as the most affordable entry‑level smart telescope that still delivers professional‑grade images.

## Why it matters
By bundling high‑quality optics, dual cameras and intelligent software into a sub‑2‑kg package, ZWO is democratizing deep‑sky astrophotography for hobbyists who previously needed bulky mounts and separate imaging rigs. The ease of use lowers the learning curve, potentially expanding the community of sky‑watchers and feeding more data into citizen‑science projects. Moreover, the competitive pricing pressures other manufacturers to innovate without inflating costs, which could accelerate the overall adoption of smart‑telescope technology.

## How to think about it
Treat the S30 Plus as a starter kit that handles target selection, framing and basic post‑processing automatically. Begin by using the app’s preset sky‑maps to locate bright nebulae and galaxies, then experiment with the dual‑band filter to see how light‑pollution suppression improves contrast. For longer exposures, let the built‑in equatorial tracking run its course, but keep sessions under ten minutes to stay within the scope’s tracking accuracy. Export the lossless FITS files when you want to apply advanced stacking or photometric analysis on a desktop.

<Callout type="warning">Never point the S30 Plus at the Sun without attaching the magnetic solar filter; improper use can cause permanent eye injury and damage the optics.</Callout>
